## Elizabeth Rider built Butler to help families turn favorite recipes into meal plans and grocery lists.

![From recipe writer to software CEO with ChatGPT Work](https://cdn.gradual.com/images/https://d2xo500swnpgl1.cloudfront.net/uploads/oaiacademy/Screenshot-2026-09-02-at-12-38-14-PM-2a78d591-9ae4-4cd5-83cf-1eb3da22d5b8-1788377901372.jpeg?fit=scale-down&width=1200)

ChatGPT helped Elizabeth Rider build  [Butler](https://butlerkitchen.com/), an AI-powered meal-planning app that takes the hardest part of feeding a family, figuring out what to cook, and turns it into a five-minute decision.

At 18 years old, Rider was walking upstairs at school when she became too tired to continue. A blood test showed a problem with her thyroid. Medication helped, but by her mid-20s, the fatigue was back. Learning to cook with real ingredients helped her manage her health and get her energy back.

In 2008, she started sharing recipes with friends and family on a free blog. Then she checked Google Analytics: her recipes were drawing 50,000 page views a month. Over the next 17 years, Rider built a business as a recipe developer, growing an audience of 2 million readers and 60,000 subscribers and guiding more than 10,000 people through healthy-eating programs.

That experience revealed a shift: we’re moving from an information age to an implementation age. Now families already know that carrots are good for them. Their problem is logistics: deciding which meal to cook, keeping track of ingredients, accommodating allergies and preferences, avoiding waste, and getting dinner on the table after a long day.

Last summer, Rider downloaded several meal-planning apps. One assigned five unfamiliar dinners in a single week, meals her family had never eaten. Others had confusing interfaces or required building meal plans and recipe catalogs from scratch. One deleted all of her saved recipes after 2 months without warning.

“No one’s going to make five new dinner recipes in a week,” she says.

Butler starts with what families already eat. It collects trusted recipes from their cookbooks, saved links and handwritten cards, including Rider’s mother’s potato salad. Users can photograph their refrigerator or pantry, match what they have to meals their family likes, account for food preferences and allergies, and generate a grocery list for what’s missing.

Then Rider faced a second logistics problem: building a software company without being a software developer.

ChatGPT became her thought partner. She modeled pricing and user growth, worked through app-store requirements, and gained the clarity and confidence to pursue the idea. During development calls, she asked ChatGPT to help her think like an expert UI/UX designer, generated interface concepts, and handed them to her engineers. Product and design decisions that once required weeks of back-and-forth could be resolved before the call ended.

“It literally saves weeks, if not months, and tens of thousands of dollars,” Rider says.

Working with three developers and a designer, she built a first version in 12 weeks and a second three months later. More than 100 people have tested the product and Rider is launching it this month. Rider estimates the traditional route would have cost five to 10 times more and taken three to four times longer, while requiring outside investors.

Instead, she and her husband bootstrapped the business.

That changes who gets to launch a company. Someone who understands a problem because she lives it every day can now build the solution, create jobs, and help other families waste less food, save money and get dinner on the table.
