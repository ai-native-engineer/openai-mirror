## Anna Matsumoto, now a master’s student at Harvard Graduate School of Design, used ChatGPT to learn the electronics behind her moving sculptures.

![Anna Matsumoto: A live performance in the audience’s hands](https://cdn.gradual.com/images/https://d2xo500swnpgl1.cloudfront.net/uploads/oaiacademy/Screenshot-2026-11-04-at-2-25-31-PM-1872d6e4-e7d8-4e7e-bfbd-d3f95fe7f3af-1793831146889.jpeg?fit=scale-down&width=1200)

“What if I can touch my performance?” Anna Matsumoto wondered. At Stanford, she made live visuals that changed with the music. In her research with blind and low-vision people, she used a 3D printer to study how shapes could convey information through touch. She wanted to know what someone might find beautiful in the feel of a shape. To build a performance people could put their hands on, she began learning electronics with ChatGPT.

As a girl growing up in Tokushima, on Japan’s Shikoku island, Matsumoto saw ads on TV for toys she couldn’t buy. A children’s show taught her how a rubber band could turn a wheel. She combined tricks like that to invent toys of her own. When she brought them to school, her classmates asked her to make things for them. “I was a magician,” she says.

Her father and older brother were doctors, and her teachers encouraged her to follow them into medicine. But on a visit to a medical school, she found herself wondering how someone had built the robot that helped surgeons operate. She told her teacher that all she wanted to do was make things.

At a hardware workshop, Matsumoto found she could follow the instructor’s directions just fine and get a motor to turn. But to improvise the way she had with her toys, she needed a deeper understanding of why the circuit worked. What was the capacitor, a component that stores electrical charge, doing there? Her instructor had a whole class to teach, and didn’t have time for all her questions. So she took pictures of the wiring and asked ChatGPT to explain what each wire did. She could keep asking basic questions for as long as she needed.

When she had a piece in mind, she would describe what she wanted it to do. “First, is it possible? And then second, what should I buy? And then third, how do I connect it?” She wired the parts together one connection at a time. When both hands were busy soldering, she could use voice mode to ask aloud what to do about the little balls forming in the solder. She usually speaks to ChatGPT in Japanese, sometimes mixing in English. She says words that imitate sounds help her describe what she sees.

Matsumoto says she can now build on her own and saves her questions for harder problems. She’d used air pumps to make a form swell and shrink like something breathing. When she described the rise and fall she wanted and asked what else might produce it, ChatGPT suggested kinds of motors she’d never considered before.

Matsumoto’s Sensora sculptures move and vibrate during a live performance. On her first prototype, balloons covering a sphere swelled with the beat. She expected people to put a hand on it and move along with the music.

Matsumoto stands beside her work during performances to watch what people do and hear what they say as they touch it. People have described the experience as meditative. Some told her it reminds them of the intensity of their feelings in childhood. At one showing, six people put their hands on the same sphere. Matsumoto watched them close their eyes.
