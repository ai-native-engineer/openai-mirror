## Stella Garber uses ChatGPT to work out what to say when her son Lev, who has ADHD, loses track of time.

![The conversations with her son that Stella Garber saves for the morning](https://cdn.gradual.com/images/https://d2xo500swnpgl1.cloudfront.net/uploads/oaiacademy/stella-photo-ceef6f3b-0ff0-4eec-8ce3-d62edc6af9f3-1793820384205.jpeg?fit=scale-down&width=1200)

When  [Stella Garber](https://www.linkedin.com/in/stellagarber/) finds her son, Lev, reading past bedtime, she turns off the light. “We’re gonna talk about this in the morning,” she tells him. The idea to leave it there, before the two of them get into an argument, came from a parenting coach she set up for herself in ChatGPT.

Lev, a deeply curious nine-year-old, was at robotics camp the day his mother described their bedtime routine. His family learned he had ADHD when he was in second grade. When Garber got calls from school about his behavior, she worried about the negative image Lev might come to believe about himself. “You don’t want your kid to think they’re a bad kid,” she says.

Garber read plenty of parenting books and found much of the advice too general. She had a five-page evaluation describing where Lev excelled and where he struggled, along with a plan his teachers used to help him at school.

Then it occurred to her that “I could do what I do for work,” she remembers thinking. A Chicago startup founder, Garber used AI to analyze transcripts. She opened a project in ChatGPT just for parenting, so its answers could draw on what Lev’s teachers saw at school and what she saw at home. After a parent-teacher conference, she adds what she learned. When she tries something at home and it falls flat, she tells the coach that, too.

In a separate chat, Garber worked with ChatGPT to write the coach’s instructions. She told it about the strict parenting she’d grown up with. A week without the iPad was a punishment she could think of on her own. She wanted a consequence Lev could actually connect to what he’d done, and a way to hold the boundary without making her son feel ashamed. She asked for “what to do in the next 30 seconds, and what to do later to prevent repeats.” At any hour, she could describe a problem out loud and ask for a few words to say to Lev, with another approach to try if the first one failed.

When Lev was late coming home from a neighbor’s house again, Garber was angry. She knew he knew when he was expected back home, and he’d faced consequences for being late before. She asked the coach what to do this time. It suggested that Lev might have lost track of time playing, then found it hard to pull himself away. When he walked in, she managed to keep her face and tone neutral, say little and then follow through later on the rules. For the next playdate, ChatGPT suggested planning a countdown. Garber says thinking it through gave her more empathy for herself as well as for Lev. She could understand why leaving and remembering to leave were hard for him and still expect him home on time.

Once Lev’s light is out, Garber uses ChatGPT to work out what to say the next morning. It sometimes gives her whole paragraphs. She tries to memorize just the parts she wants to say, though she sometimes keeps the words open on her phone. The overnight pause she takes gives Lev time to prepare, too. By morning he knows a consequence is coming. His mother has had time to choose one she can explain, and he’s calm enough to hear her.
