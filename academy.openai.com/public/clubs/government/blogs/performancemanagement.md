# Thought Leadership: The end of performance reviews written from memory

## AI can make performance management more fair, continuous, and evidence-based by helping people document the full arc of their work, not just remember it at review time.

![Thought Leadership: The end of performance reviews written from memory](https://cdn.gradual.com/images/https://d2xo500swnpgl1.cloudfront.net/uploads/oaiacademy/ChatGPT-Academy-Cover-2--3d36e529-8d94-43a5-bd35-49a72ef41ed5-1783520620298.jpeg?fit=scale-down&width=1200)

Every year, organizations conduct one of the most important exercises in leadership: evaluating talent. Managers write performance reviews. Employees document accomplishments. Promotion committees debate impact. Leadership teams identify future leaders. Yet despite decades of investment in performance management systems, most organizations still rely on a surprisingly fragile foundation:

Memory.

Managers are asked to summarize twelve months of work from recollection. Employees scramble to remember accomplishments from six months ago. Individuals who depart the organization take with them valuable legacy context of what their peers contributed. Positive feedback shared in a meeting disappears into a chat thread. Important moments of leadership fade with time.

The irony is that modern organizations have never had more evidence of performance. They simply haven’t had a practical way to capture it.

﻿ [Most conversations about AI and performance management focus on writing reviews faster.](https://hbr.org/2026/05/gen-ai-could-fix-performance-reviews-or-make-them-even-worse) That’s the wrong conversation. The real opportunity is much bigger. AI isn’t going to transform performance management because it can write reviews. AI is going to transform performance management because it can build evidence.

*What If Great Work Never Had to Become Invisible?*

Throughout my career working in technology companies, government, and more than two decades of military service, I’ve seen the same problem repeat itself.

Organizations want to reward impact. But impact is often difficult to see. The employee who quietly influences a major decision. The project manager who prevents a problem before it occurs. The teammate who mentors others behind the scenes. The individual contributor who consistently creates value without drawing attention to themselves. Too often, these contributions disappear.

Not because they weren’t important. Because nobody captured them, an employee is too humble to remind a reviewer, or the person in charge left mid-year without documenting the success of their teammates. As a result, many performance reviews become storytelling exercises based on memory rather than evidence-based assessments. Who can best remember their accomplishments? Who kept the best notes? Who is most comfortable advocating for themselves?

Those are not necessarily the same people creating the most value.

*The AI-Generated Evidence Portfolio*

Imagine a different approach. Instead of waiting until the end of the quarter or year, every employee uses AI to review their work each week. I personally use an AI agent that reviews my daily activity including my calendar events, meeting notes, and project artifacts to draft a summary of my output.

At the end of every Friday, it reviews the daily summaries and then writes a summary based on:

* What was accomplished?

* Who was influenced?

* What outcomes were achieved?

* What positive feedback was received?

* What constructive criticism changed my approach?

* What skills were demonstrated?

* What lessons were learned?

* How did I show up for others, or who showed up for me?

Those weekly summaries roll into monthly reports. (see below for prompt) Monthly reports roll into quarterly reports. Quarterly reports roll into evidence for annual reviews. Suddenly, performance management no longer depends on memory. It depends on evidence. Employees are no longer trying to reconstruct the past. They can bring the receipts.

This is where the real transformation begins.

For decades, organizations have treated performance reviews as snapshots which lends itself to recency bias. AI allows them to become documentaries. What is the story your weekly reviews tell? More important, when we are conscious about how we are showing up, we are more likely to fulfill the role in a way that brings us pride and purpose.

The implications are significant. Performance reviews become more accurate. Promotion packets become stronger. Career conversations become grounded in facts rather than recollections. And perhaps most importantly, great work no longer is forgotten.

*From Activity to Impact*

In addition to cataloging and researching, AI can assist leaders and an employee’s ability to document. One of the most common mistakes employees make is documenting activity instead of impact.

They write:

“Led AI training.”

Or:

“Managed project implementation.”

Or:

“Supported leadership initiatives.”

Those statements describe effort. Leaders evaluate outcomes.

Activity is not the same thing as progress, but progress can be hard to gauge, when it happens over long periods. The intentionality about capturing, reflecting and holding myself accountable for progress also means I am more likely to be accurately pursuing the north star of impact and in DOW terms “Commanders Intent”.

The stronger narrative is:

“Designed and delivered an AI adoption program reaching more than 500 employees, accelerating tool adoption across three business units and reducing content creation time by 30 percent.”

Same work. Different story.

This approach to performance reviews also has a secondary benefit: resume.

When and if you are considering moving to a different role or organization the previous narrative would be equally relevant in my performance review as it would be on my resume. Employers are looking for real, tangible proof of not only AI use, but more importantly, of AI impact.

Historically, employees have struggled to make this translation because they only think about it during performance review season. AI changes that. Instead of asking employees once a year to explain their value, AI can continuously connect activities to business outcomes surfacing in real time if all teammates are working towards the same objectives. The result is a living record of impact rather than a last-minute attempt to assemble one. This affords employee an evidence based portfolio when they need to articulate impact on their CV, LinkedIn profile, or in an interview. The AI portfolio is a tool for employee empowerment, capturing work that potentially might go unnoticed.

*The Next Frontier: Skill-Based Performance Maps*

There is another shift coming that may prove even more important. As employees increasingly use AI in their daily workflows and in providing summaries of their accomplishments, organizations gain a new source of insight. Not just outcomes. Patterns.

AI can help identify how people work, and where they might be having outsized impact in areas that they are not typically credited for.

Which employees consistently synthesize complex information into executive-ready recommendations? Which employees demonstrate strong strategic thinking? Who excels at stakeholder management? Who is becoming more effective at problem-solving? Who is automating repetitive tasks to focus on higher-value work? Where are people spending unnecessary effort? Where might additional training create disproportionate gains? Who is the go-to person when there are challenges? Who sets the “Vibe and Tribe” of your organizational norms?”

In other words, AI makes dynamic and even “soft” skill maps more obtainable. The best managers are coaches. Coaches need data. AI may provide the richest developmental dataset organizations have ever possessed. Now it will be possible to understand at scale what an organization’s needs and gaps are, who on the team is a generalist vs. a specialist, where upskilling is needed or if it makes sense to bring in external talent. It will allow leaders to figure out faster how they can create greater alignment on their teams.

Leaders can now also ask questions that would have been impossible to prove the answers to before…”Which of the team works the most cross-functionally” “Who is invited to the most engagements”, “Who actually spends the most time with our customers (hint-it usually isn’t who you think) “Who’s calendar shows bandwidth to take on something new?”

AI powered evaluation processes create continuous feedback loops. The most effective leaders provide guidance early, reinforce strengths often, and address development opportunities while they are still actionable.  [According to the Society for Human Resource Management, 28% of employees report not receiving timely feedback.](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/topics/performance-management) Using AI strategically will free up capacity to allow leaders to focus on mentoring and shaping behavior rather than simply identifying it. Even with an AI infused process, the manager-employee relationship will always remain the most critical factor. AI acts as an assistant that creates more time for deep, human to human coaching, rather than replacing it.

The majority of AI users use less than 10% of the AI’s capability. One of the ways to be intentional with your AI tool is to use the personalization, memory, or instruction features to make it clear to the AI what the definition of success is, or where you want to get stronger or be more intentional. If your goal is to get promoted you can tell your tool to remember that is your goal, you can personalize how you want to tool to be proactive with you in that journey and you can use instructions to reinforce the way you navigate human-machine teaming and use AI not only to help you reach your organizational goals, but to help you reach your own goals and potential.

Examples:

I tell ChatGPT the 3 things that I want to be true about my “brand” in the organization, meaning how would I want others to describe me. Then an instruction might be, to have it correct you if you aren’t being true to a core value, or going “off-brand”. For example, if I want to be known for being considered and intentional (non-biased) in my thinking, I might instruct the AI to always give me both sides of an issue, policy or initiative so I have empathy and understanding for someone who might see something different than I do. Or if I want to be known as creative, I might tell the AI to “Always give me 3 unusual, or unexpected takes on a query, prompt or activity”. If I have great gut instincts but want to be known for being evidence driven, I might instruct ChatGPT to “always bring facts and sources that are respected/official, give me the direct link to the source, and remind me to cross-check before publishing.”

There are also critical psychological elements of taking time to document your journey. High performing teams rarely take the time to celebrate a win, big or small, and to process progress.  [But research by Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer analyzed 12,000 diary entries and found that making and noting progress in meaningful work was the single biggest factor in boosting emotions, motivations, and perceptions of performance.](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=40692) High-performing teams don't wait until the finish line to celebrate. They mark progress along the way because recognizing wins fuels the energy, confidence, and resilience needed to achieve the next one.

Performance reviews should not be an afterthought or an exercise that injects surprise or uncertainty. Both the leader and the employee should feel a performance review is a continuation of a year long conversation and relationship. This model only works if it is employee-directed. The employee should know what sources are being reviewed, be able to edit or exclude material, and control what ultimately gets shared with a manager or formal review process.

As a leader in an organization, you can account for the invisible work, reduce recency bias and uneven self advocacy while having better coaching conversations. The opportunity to implement AI first behavior in the performance management process can create meaningful change you will see beyond annual reviews. But maybe most importantly, documenting your journey isn’t just about getting promoted, it is about learning from where you have been to inform where you are going.
